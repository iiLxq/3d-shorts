## Fact sheet (verified)
- Air Canada Flight 143, **23 July 1983**, Boeing 767, **61 passengers + 8 crew**. Fuel ran out at **41,000 ft** over Red Lake, Ontario. Cause: fuel density 1.77 **lb**/L used as if it were **kg**/L → less than half the fuel needed. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gimli_Glider))
- With no engines most screens went blank; the **ram air turbine (RAT)** drove a hydraulic pump that powered the flight controls. Glided ~17 min at roughly 12:1. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gimli_Glider), [FAA AD 757/767 RAT](https://www.gpo.gov/fdsys/pkg/FR-1996-09-30/html/96-24650.htm))
- Captain **Bob Pearson** was an experienced **glider pilot**; F/O Maurice Quintal suggested the former RCAF base at **Gimli**, then a motorsport park with a **car race** running. Nose gear collapsed; **no serious injuries**.
- The jet was repaired and **retired in 2008** (≈ 25 more years of flying).

## VO only

If both engines on your plane die, a tiny windmill is what keeps you flying.

A jet with no engines doesn't fall like a rock. It glides — more than ten feet forward for every foot down. But no engines means no power. The screens go dark... and the controls steering this giant glider run on hydraulic pressure — that's draining away.

The yoke gets heavier. Here's what saves you.

A hatch in the belly pops open, and a little propeller drops into the wind. It spins up, drives a pump, and pushes pressure back into the controls. Steering's back. A pinwheel out a car window — powered by your own speed.

In 1983, Air Canada 143 ran dry at forty-one thousand feet — someone mixed up pounds and kilograms. Luckily, the captain was a glider pilot. Unluckily, the runway they picked had a car race on it. He landed anyway. No serious injuries.

And the plane? It kept flying for almost twenty-five more years.
